Key Population Concepts & Global Trends

Demography Definitions

  • Demographers: Experts who study human populations and how they change over time.
  • Crude Birth Rate (CBR): Number of live births per 1,000 people per year.
  • Crude Death Rate (CDR): Number of deaths per 1,000 people per year.
  • Total Fertility Rate (TFR): Average number of children a woman has during her lifetime.
  • Natural Increase: Calculated by subtracting CDR from CBR (CBR - CDR).
  • Net Migration: Difference between immigration (inflow) and emigration (outflow).
  • Population Momentum: Continued growth despite falling birth rates due to a large percentage of youth entering child-bearing years.

Global Trends & Drivers

Milestones: Reached 8 billion in Nov 2022 (up from 6 billion in 1999). Projected to reach 9.8 billion around 2050.

Disparities: Almost all future growth will occur in LICs / Less Developed Countries. High-Income Countries (HICs) face flat or declining populations.

Falling Birth Rates: Women's rights/education, lower infant mortality, widespread contraception access, high child-rearing costs in HICs.

Falling Death Rates: Medical advancements, better healthcare access, disease treatments, improved sanitation and clean water.

Demographic Transition Model (DTM) & Ageing Populations

The 5 Stages of DTM

Stage 1 High Stationary

High birth & high death rates. Very low or zero population growth. Reliance on agricultural labor, high infant mortality.

Stage 2 Early Expanding

High birth rate continues; death rate drops rapidly due to healthcare & sanitation. Rapid population growth.

Stage 3 Late Expanding

Birth rate drops rapidly (female education/contraception); death rate remains low. Population growth slows.

Stage 4 Low Stationary

Low birth & low death rates. Stable, post-industrial population supported by high female employment.

Stage 5 Declining

TFR drops below replacement rate (<2.1). Population ages and slowly contracts (e.g., Japan, Germany).

DTM Strengths & Weaknesses

Strengths: Accurately models historical European demographic development and provides a global baseline.

Weaknesses: Eurocentric; ignores migration impacts (e.g., Germany's growth via immigration); ignores sudden global shocks (pandemics/war); doesn't fit rapid drops in developing nations.

Population Pyramids & Cohorts

  • • Young Dependents: Ages 0–14
  • • Economically Active: Ages 15–64
  • • Elderly Dependents: Ages 65+
  • • Wide Base (LIC): High birth rate, high youth dependency ratio (e.g. Somalia).
  • • Narrow Base / Column (HIC): Aging population, low birth rate (e.g. UK).
  • • Bulges & Indentations: Inward indentations show wars/epidemics; outward bulges show migration inflows or baby booms.

Impacts of an Ageing Population

Positive Impacts

  • • Experienced older adults remain in workforce longer & volunteer.
  • • Provide vital informal childcare for working parents.
  • • Boost leisure, silver tourism, and accessible urban sectors.
  • • Lower overall resource consumption reduces environmental strain.

Negative Impacts

  • • Massive pension & healthcare costs (e.g., Japan spends 40% of public budget on elderly).
  • • Shrinking active labor force hampers economic output.
  • • Higher tax burden placed on a smaller working-age population.
  • • Extreme heatwaves/floods heavily impact vulnerable elderly populations.

Youthful Population & Case Study: Hungary

Youthful Population Structures

Prevalent in LICs with high birth rates and high youth populations (<15 years old).

Benefits:

Large cheap future labor pool attracting investments, expanding domestic consumer markets, and providing a long-term tax base.

Challenges:

High youth dependency ratio strains healthcare and schools. Risk of food/water shortages, deforestation for fuelwood, and desertification.

Deep Dive: Hungary's Pro-Natalist Strategy

Drivers of Decline: Post-communist economic shift delayed marriage; female higher education reached 36% (2020); 2004 EU entry triggered outward migration to Western Europe. TFR hit a record low of 1.23 in 2011.
Government Incentive Package (~5% of GDP):
  • • CSOK Housing Subsidy: $50,000–$80,000 grant for couples with 3+ kids buying new homes (avg annual salary is $11k–$15k).
  • • Tax Exemptions: Lifetime income tax exemption for mothers raising 4 or more children.
  • • Loan Forgiveness: Subsidized loans fully written off upon having a 3rd child.
  • • Social Supports: Free textbooks (first 9 years), free/discounted transit, and 'Women 40' pension scheme for grandmothers.

Key Migration Terminology

International Migration: Movement across national borders to establish permanent residence.
Emigrant vs Immigrant: Emigrant = leaving home country; Immigrant = entering target country.
Voluntary Migration: Economic migrants moving by choice for jobs, better income, or sending remittances.
Forced Migration: Unwilling relocation due to war, persecution, or environmental disaster.
Refugee vs Asylum Seeker: Refugee has legal status under international law; Asylum seeker is awaiting legal decision.
Push vs Pull Factors: Push = negative home conditions; Pull = attractive destination qualities.

Push/Pull Factors & Migration Management

Push Factors (Origin)

  • • High unemployment or low wages
  • • Lack of healthcare, schools, or basic infrastructure
  • • Civil war, political instability, or human rights abuses
  • • Famines, droughts, or natural disaster risks

Pull Factors (Destination)

  • • Abundant job opportunities & higher wages
  • • Superior public healthcare and education systems
  • • Political safety, rule of law, and personal freedom
  • • Lower exposure to environmental hazards & climate risks

Destination Country Impacts

Positives:

Fills labor gaps, rejuvenates aging demographics, increases tax receipts, enriches culture.

Negatives:

Strains schools, housing & healthcare; potential social tensions if integration fails.

Origin Country Impacts

Positives:

Inflow of remittances boosts local economy; returning migrants bring new skills.

Negatives:

Brain Drain (loss of doctors/engineers/youth); leaves behind an aging, dependent population.

Global Migration Control Strategies

Deterrence Measures

• US–Mexico Border: Physical fences + virtual towers (sensors/cameras). Desert terrain makes total coverage costly.

• UK Illegal Migration Act 2023: Detained/deported small boat arrivals (Rwanda plan ruled illegal & scrapped in 2024).

Points-Based Systems

• Singapore (COMPASS): Evaluates foreign workers across 4 criteria: Salary, Qualifications, Diversity, and Local Employment Ratio. Requires 40 points to pass.

Promoting Emigration

• Pakistan: Government actively supports outward labor migration to secure high remittance income and incoming foreign exchange.

Bilateral Case Study: Mexico to USA Migration

World's largest bilateral migration corridor. ~11 million Mexican immigrants residing in the USA in 2024. Shares a 3,139 km physical border.

Mexico (Push Factors & Stats)

• GNI Per Capita: $10,820 (2022)

• Doctor Ratio: 1,800 people per doctor

• Adult Literacy Rate: 55%

• Push Factors: Drug cartel violence/kidnappings, high rural poverty among small farm workers, limited job market.

USA (Pull Factors & Stats)

• GNI Per Capita: $76,370 (2022)

• Doctor Ratio: 400 people per doctor

• Adult Literacy Rate: 99%

• Pull Factors: Higher wages in construction, agriculture & hospitality; safety, strong infrastructure & existing family networks.

Impacts on Mexico:

• Positive:

Sends home $60 billion annually in remittances (Mexico's 2nd largest income source).

• Negative:

Depletes rural working males; leaves an aging population and dependent families.

Impacts on the USA:

• Positive:

Fills essential labor shortages in low-wage sectors; enriches culture and cuisine.

• Negative:

Spurs political debate on wage suppression, public service spending, and vigilante border patrols (Minutemen).

Historical US Policy & Border Timeline

Era / Policy Actions & Key Outcomes
1942–1964: Bracero Program Encouraged agricultural contract labor during WWII; brought over 5 million Mexican workers to the US.
1965 & 1986 Acts 1986 IRCA granted legal status to long-term undocumented residents while introducing penalties for employers hiring unauthorized workers.
1990s Enforcement Stricter border penalties inadvertently increased reliance on human coyotes/smugglers and discouraged seasonal return trips.
2002–2016 Enforcement Constructed 850 miles of physical border walls, doubled Border Patrol agents, and executed over 1.2M deportations.
Post-2020 Trends Shift towards temporary legal work visas; economic shifts and stricter border checks saw rising net-zero or return migration to Mexico.
